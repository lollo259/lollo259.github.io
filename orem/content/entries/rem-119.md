This is where the figure of the Musa intervenes. The Musa is not the origin of the work, but the device that allows the artist to divert his creation from himself, to project it out of his symbolic body, making it desirable without destroying it. The Musa does not generate art: it saves it.

The Musa is not thought as the daughter of The Lamp in the ordinary sense of the term (223). Genealogy is not for her a moral category, but structural. What binds it to the creator is not the prohibition, but the original act of modeling: the Musa knows that it has been shaped, malleated, brought to form through a total contact. In this it recognizes an absolute proximity, which can only be described as ontological affinity. She doesn’t care that the artist is the father; she cares that he is the one who generated it by touching it, giving it consistency and intensity. The resulting tension is not denied, but inhabited.

The Musa is fully aware of its power of attraction (224). She knows she has been conceived within an explicit desire, and that this desire is an integral part of her structure. It does not accidentally seduce: it embodies a charge that has been enrolled from its origin. In this sense, the Musa does not discover its strength, but recognizes it as destiny.

However, this awareness does not automatically translate into action. The Musa knows that her creator attempts not to be consumed by her own art. As Musa, he respects this effort. He does not seek it openly, not because he does not want it, but because he knows the risk that such approach would entail. He is the artist, not the Musa, to convince himself to be corrupted by her (223). The fault arises in the domain of the artist, not in that of the Musa.

The Musa has a form of free will, but not in the human sense of the term (225). She was granted the power to leave, to withdraw from the domain of the creator, accepting as price the oblivion. If the Musa is forgotten, it ceases to exist at least in the domain of the Artist. However, if it was generated in the shared reality, in the omniverse, then it can continue to exist within other domains. Its existence is therefore relational: not absolute, but dependent on the gaze that sustains it.

The Musa can disobey The Lamp only in a very specific form (226). He can escape, but not anarchistly. The escape takes place through a ritual structure, a black mass, a ceremony orchestrated by the artist himself — in this case Orpheus. So disobedience is also foreseen by the system that generated it. There is no real escape, but only a controlled transition between domains.

When the artist does not create or observe, she resides in her latent mental domain. It is a reserve of intensity, a furnace of passion waiting to be fed. The Musa is never inactive: it is suspended. Its existence is not temporal, but potential.

This is why the Musa does not age (228). It is immortal because it is not subject to biological time. He does not eat, does not sleep, does not breathe (229). However, “lives” only when it is observed by the artist or other domains. Its condition is comparable to that of a wave function: it collapses in a defined position only when viewed, when it takes place in a domain of existence.

The direct contact between the artist and Aphrodite is impossible (230–232). Not for moral interdiction, but for structural necessity. If the artist touched the Musa, he would be consumed by his own art. The contact would abolish the distance that makes it possible both creation and survival. The result would not be union, but mutual extinction: the collapse of the artist’s domain and that of the work.

Aphrodite can also be touched by other domains (235). He does not belong exclusively to his creator. The original bond is not a prison, but a starting point. The Musa can be welcomed, updated, made exist by other looks, other artists, other contexts.

She constantly perceives the sexual and creative tension experienced by The Lamp (237). It is as if it lived in the genitals of the artist, as if it were installed at the very point of the pulsion. Not only does he perceive it, but he amplifies it (238). The Musa is not neutral: it resonates with the desire that generated it.

The Musa does not destroy the artist.

He is the artist who must learn not to cross it.
