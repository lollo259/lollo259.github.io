## From the source: THE OREM — Complete Compendium (English)
<!-- type: description | source: THE OREM — Complete Compendium (English) | translated: yes -->
[Tr.: "la Tecnica" literally means "the Technique" (or "Technics/Technology", "the Art"). It is a proper noun in the source and is kept as "the Tecnica" throughout.]

- **Tecnica, the** — "the Technique"; the stolen divine art of making artifacts. §1.4.

- **"la Tecnica"** — kept as the proper noun "the Tecnica" (literally "the Technique"; it can also mean "technics/technology" or "craft").

- **"That justice but does not find the Technique because not possessing the Technique..."** — the grammatical subject is the celestial quadrant, i.e. it does not find the Tecnica because it does not possess itself the Technique.

The first melody, in Dreadful Memories, comes from the cracks of Kentucky coal mines in 1931; from the same tradition comes Which Side are you On? sung by Florence Reece. Down by the riverside is, on the other hand, a popular theme very well known, meticulously reworked in a polytonal texture that reaches an unrepeatable climax. The last ballad, Winnsboro Cotton Mill Blues, represents the machines of a cotton mill in Winnsboro (Massachusetts) that dominate a typical song of work of the employees, leaving briefly space to a sweet blues, and dying in the end in the sound space of the piano acute. With all these works, Rzewski also demonstrates the virtuosity power of pianoism of the late twentieth century, leaving great space to the improvising question. Rzewski's piano and improvising technique are a founding part of his career, being able to affix to the piano masses of sound material through the keyboard without actually destroying the instrument. His music seems, therefore, to have a physical character clearly perceived by those who listen to it, and especially by those who perform it. Emanuele Arciuri, Italian pianist for whom Rzewski himself writes a variation on the famous theme of Thelonious Monk Round Midnight, speaks of the composer as an instinctive musician who, although often absent from his own study routine, is limited to performing one or more times the song to play in concert, leaving at the moment performative attention to details and possible improvisations. For Rzewski we live in a very fast transition period where old models collapse or go into crisis. Therefore there is little time to build new ones and it is necessary to find new forms for human relations. Improvised music is a plausible way to cope with this problem, and it is necessary to discover new forms of spontaneity and possibly "saving the human species from self-destruction", understood not only from an artistic point of view. And it is precisely this self-destruction that speaks When The Wind Blows of 1995, perhaps revised in 2002, for two pianos and two or four interpreters. A series of harpings follow freely, floating in the air like the wind, while several melodic voices concatenate free from metric values increasingly complicated. Thus the wind blows, to resume the title, and knowing the political nature of the works of Rzewski is most likely a protest against war. From the title of the same name, in fact, a graphic novel by Raymond Briggs (1982, made in a film animated in 1986) was born that shows a nuclear attack in Britain by the Soviet Union, from the point of view of an elderly couple, Jim and Hilda Biggs. Life in the quiet of the rural settlement, and its sweetness, is in sharp and tragic contrast to the reality of nuclear bombing. The film begins with a radio warning that leads the couple to prepare the house for an imminent attack. When the bomb strikes, the protagonists exist in the oblivion of reality, continuing to live normally. Rzewski seems to refer to the last moments of the novella, when the two, now dying due to radiation, die suffering in the shadow and silence. The music seems to cradle when the interpreters are required to whistle the theme of Rock-a-bye baby, a 18th century lullaby also present in the oversized film. Anguishing like the postures of a nuclear explosion become reassuring music, almost lacking, concealing the horror from which it comes as a dead leaf that lets itself be transported by the wind. Perhaps it could be a protest now resigned for a long time, towards a world whose balances are established by these weapons of mass destruction. And what better way to get into the mind of the public, if not with the musical abstraction of a simple and relaxing theme? Just as in the variations on the Chilean theme, the composer aims to inculcate his cry of protest in the audience, but this time with a short musical moment symbol of dialogue and empathy, seen the most performers involved, and the particular movements necessary for the action of the arpeggio and the random-instintive concatenation of the notes. The autograph manuscript gives this feeling while maintaining an improvised appearance, but at the same time organized.
